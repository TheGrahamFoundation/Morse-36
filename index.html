THE QUESTION
Machines are surprisingly verbose with each other.

We routinely transmit JSON, XML, schemas, field names and repeated descriptions even when communicating systems already share context. Morse/36 explores a narrower question than general-purpose compression: how much representation can disappear when two machines share deterministic semantics?

WATCH THE IDEA

Layer by layer.

1 · SourceJSON, HL7, XML, YAML or language. Keep the original representation where it belongs.
2 · FTIPNormalize the source into canonical semantic intent: the meaning that actually needs to survive.
3 · Morse → Morse²Map semantic atoms to shared words, then substitute recurring combinations with higher-order words.
4 · M36Pack the resulting words inside a bounded 36-character lexical envelope for transport.
PACKING EXPERIMENTS

36 characters. Different vocabularies.

The current Workbench explores an M36/3 profile with up to twelve 3-character words and an M36/2 profile with up to eighteen 2-character Base36 words. These are experimental packing profiles, not a frozen wire standard. The point is to expose the tradeoff between vocabulary size, composition and density.

BREAK THE ASSUMPTIONS

This is a hypothesis, not a victory lap.

Morse/36 is not claiming arbitrary information can magically fit into 36 characters. Shared vocabularies cost something. Registries cost something. High-entropy information still has to travel or be referenced. Try your own payload, inspect every morsing layer, and find where the abstraction breaks.

OPEN RESEARCH

What still has to be proven.

Word grammar, FTIP canonicalization, registry economics, collisions, context negotiation, failure behavior, security, conformance and reproducible comparisons with compressed JSON, CBOR, Protocol Buffers and other appropriate alternatives. Benchmark 001 exists to measure the idea rather than manufacture a win.

The Workbench currently visualizes experimental deterministic mappings. Its byte reduction excludes registry distribution, negotiation and transport overhead. Nothing here should yet be treated as a stable production wire standard.
